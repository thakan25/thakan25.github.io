## India is checking who is allowed to vote

India is the world's largest democracy, with nearly a billion registered voters. Since 2025, its Election Commission has been re-checking the voter list door to door, state by state. The process is called the **Special Intensive Revision (SIR)**.

On paper, that's sensible: voter lists collect people who have died or moved. But the way it was done puts the burden on the voter. If an official couldn't find you, you were removed. If a computer found something odd in your family details, you had to prove with documents that you belong on the list.

I looked closely at one state, **West Bengal** (about 100 million people), where the data is detailed enough to check, seat by seat.

## 9 million names removed in five months

Between November 2025 and April 2026, **9.1 million people**, about **1 in 8 voters**, were removed from West Bengal's list.

- **5.8 million** were dropped because their form didn't come back: listed as dead, moved, absent or duplicate. They got **no notice and no hearing**.
- **6 million** more were flagged by software and had to prove their eligibility. Judges then ruled **2.7 million** of them not eligible.


## Who was removed

This is the heart of it. **Muslims are 27% of West Bengal's people, but 70% of those removed after hearings.** That's about 2.6 times their share: roughly 1.9 million of the 2.7 million.

It isn't explained by where Muslims live. In every kind of area, from the least Muslim to the most, Muslims were removed far beyond their share. Even where they are only 6% of the population, they were 25% of those removed.

Seat by seat, it's the same. If removals matched population, every dot below would sit on the dashed line. Instead, **285 of 290 constituencies** sit above it, and in 214 Muslims were removed at **double their share or more**.

### Where it starts

I expected unfairness at every step. It wasn't at every step, which makes where it appears more striking. Removing the dead and the moved was **not** biased, and the judges were, if anything, more lenient with Muslim voters. The bias came from the **computer flagging**: in mostly-Muslim areas, **21%** of voters were flagged, against **3%** in areas with few Muslims.

<figure class="card">
<img src="/assets/images/bengal-sir/skew-starts-at-flagging.jpg" alt="In areas with few Muslims, 3% of voters were flagged; in mostly-Muslim areas, 21%" loading="lazy">
</figure>

What rules the software used, and why they caught so many more Muslims, has never been explained.

## Removed before the election, heard after it

People who were removed can appeal. About **3.8 million** appeals were filed with just 19 tribunals. By September, only about **3%** had been decided. When cases are heard, **8 or 9 in 10** people win and are put back on the list.

But India's Supreme Court ruled that **you can't vote while your appeal is waiting**. The state election in April 2026 went ahead with almost all of them still waiting. A High Court judge remarked that, at this pace, the appeals could take **21 years** to clear.

It took five months to remove millions of people. Putting the wrongly removed ones back may take decades.

## Who is responsible

India's Constitution makes the **Election Commission** responsible for voter lists. It ordered this revision, set its rules, and set up the appeals.

The doubts come from inside too. In September, a leading Indian newspaper reported that the Commission's own two other members had [formally objected at least 14 times](https://www.outlookindia.com/national/why-two-election-commissioners-objected-to-sir-exercises-under-gyanesh-kumar) to how the revision was being run. The Commission [says](https://www.deccanchronicle.com/amp/nation/current-affairs/all-decisions-taken-in-unanimity-eci-denies-rift-over-sir-1989814) all its decisions were lawful and unanimous.

## The people protesting are being detained

Since late September, young people, students, farmers and opposition parties have protested across India, asking the Chief Election Commissioner to resign and the revision to be reviewed.

They have been met with orders to stop gathering, issued in the name of keeping public order:

- Before a rally in New Delhi on 10 October, police [refused permission](https://thewire.in/government/delhi-police-deny-permission-to-oct-10-rally-order-closure-of-57-metro-stations) and sought to shut **57 metro stations**. On the day, **thousands of protesters were detained** ([reports](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2026_anti-CEC_protests) range from about 2,000 to over 5,000).
- The main protest site in the capital was then removed from the list of places where protest is allowed, and two youth leaders were [jailed for five days](https://www.khaleejtimes.com/world/asia/top-indian-cockroach-leaders-jail-trespassing-jantar-mantar-saurav-das-ashutosh-ranka) for trespassing there.
- On 23 October, another march to the Election Commission was stopped and [protesters detained](https://www.indiatvnews.com/news/india/several-protesters-detained-as-anti-sir-march-heads-towards-eci-headquarters-section-163-enforced-2026-10-23-1057748) under an order banning gatherings.

## Why I'm standing here

I'm an Indian living in Japan. I believe in rules, and I started this expecting to find the whole process was rigged. It wasn't: most removals were routine.

But a democracy is only as fair as its voter list. When a computer decides who must prove they belong, when it falls hardest on one religious minority, when the fix comes after the vote, and when people who ask questions are detained, that is worth standing up for, even from far away.
